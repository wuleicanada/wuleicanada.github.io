War and Peace by Leo Tolstoy: A Summary

War and Peace (1869) by Leo Tolstoy is one of the longest and most celebrated novels in world literature. The text at Project Gutenberg #2600 is the English translation by Louise and Aylmer Maude. Set against the backdrop of Napoleon’s campaigns against Russia between 1805 and 1812, the novel follows five aristocratic families through fifteen books and two epilogues, weaving together hundreds of characters and Tolstoy’s own philosophical meditations on history, free will, and the nature of power.

Overview

The novel opens in July 1805 at a St. Petersburg soirée hosted by Anna Pávlovna Schérer, where talk of Napoleon’s conquests dominates polite conversation. From this glittering drawing-room Tolstoy expands outward to military campaigns, country estates, Moscow ballrooms, a burning city, and the frozen roads of retreat — before drawing everything together in the quiet domesticity of the epilogue.

Main Characters

  • Pierre Bezukhov — An illegitimate count who unexpectedly inherits a vast fortune. Idealistic, awkward, and perpetually searching for meaning, Pierre passes through a disastrous marriage, Freemasonry, a duel, a failed attempt to assassinate Napoleon, and imprisonment before finding peace.
  • Prince Andrei Bolkonsky — A brilliant, proud, and melancholy officer. He fights at Austerlitz, recovers from near-fatal wounds, falls in love with Natasha, is wounded again at Borodino, and dies slowly during the French retreat.
  • Natasha Rostova — The vibrant, impulsive daughter of Count Rostov. She is the emotional heart of the novel: enchanting at her first ball, nearly ruined by an elopement scheme, and ultimately transformed by grief into a devoted wife and mother.
  • Princess Mary Bolkonskaya — Andrei’s deeply religious sister. Devoted to her tyrannical father and her faith, she eventually finds love with Nicholas Rostov after the old prince’s death.
  • Nicholas Rostov — Natasha’s elder brother, a straightforward cavalry officer who loves horses, hunting, and later Princess Mary. His family’s financial ruin forces him to choose duty over romance.
  • Helene Kuragina — Pierre’s beautiful, cold, and scheming wife, a fixture of Petersburg society whose machinations contribute to several characters’ misfortunes.
  • Anatole Kuragin — Helene’s reckless brother, who nearly destroys Natasha by persuading her to elope with him.
  • Kutuzov — The elderly Russian commander-in-chief, presented by Tolstoy as an embodiment of patient, passive wisdom — the antithesis of Napoleon’s self-styled genius.

Plot Summary by Book

Books One–Three: 1805 — The Austrian Campaign

Russia enters the war against Napoleon as part of the Third Coalition. Prince Andrei joins the staff of General Kutuzov and sees action at the skirmish of Schöngraben. At the catastrophic Battle of Austerlitz, Andrei carries the Russian standard forward and is gravely wounded. Lying on the field, gazing up at the infinite sky, he experiences a spiritual revelation that humbles his earlier ambitions. Pierre, meanwhile, arrives in St. Petersburg after his father’s death and is immediately swept up in schemes over the inheritance and, manipulated by Prince Vasili, into marrying the beautiful Helene.

Book Four: 1806 — Return Home

Nicholas Rostov returns home on leave with his friend Denisov. The Rostov household overflows with warmth: Natasha, Sonya, and Petya are growing up. Pierre discovers Helene’s infidelity and fights a duel with her admirer Dolokhov, wounding him. The marriage collapses.

Book Five: 1806–07 — Pierre and the Freemasons

Pierre, adrift and tormented by questions of purpose and meaning (“What is life, and what is death? What power governs all?”), is drawn into Freemasonry. He attempts to improve conditions on his serfs’ estates and sets off on a spiritual quest. Andrei, believed dead, has returned to his estate at Bald Hills, where his wife Lise dies in childbirth leaving a son, little Nicholas. Andrei sinks into depression and withdraws from public life.

Book Six: 1808–10 — Peace and Stagnation

The Peace of Tilsit has brought a lull. Andrei, slowly rekindled by an encounter with an oak tree blooming again in spring, travels to the Rostov estate of Otradnoe, where he hears Natasha singing by an open window at night and falls in love. Pierre grows disillusioned with Freemasonry. Nicholas Rostov, heavily in debt, considers marrying a rich heiress but cannot bring himself to abandon his attachment to Sonya.

Books Seven–Eight: 1810–12 — Engagement and Betrayal

Andrei proposes to Natasha; at the insistence of his tyrannical father, they agree to wait a year before marrying. During Andrei’s absence abroad, Natasha is dazzled by the charming Anatole Kuragin at the opera and agrees to elope with him. Pierre discovers the scheme and stops it, but the engagement with Andrei is broken. Natasha falls seriously ill with shame and despair. Andrei is devastated.

Books Nine–Ten: 1812 — Invasion and Borodino

Napoleon crosses the Russian frontier in June 1812 with over half a million men. Tolstoy pauses the narrative to reflect on the impossibility of assigning a single cause to such events. The French advance is inexorable. On the eve of the Battle of Borodino, Pierre wanders the Russian lines and watches soldiers pray before the icon of the Black Madonna. During the battle itself — the bloodiest day of the campaign — Pierre stands on a battery under fire, bewildered and terrified, witnessing the carnage at close quarters. Prince Andrei, commanding a regiment, is torn by a bursting shell and mortally wounded.

Books Eleven–Thirteen: 1812 — Moscow Burns

Kutuzov decides to abandon Moscow without a final battle. The city empties; the Rostovs flee, loading their carts with wounded soldiers rather than their own possessions. Moscow is set ablaze — partly by accident, partly by design. Napoleon enters a ghost city. Pierre remains behind, disguised as a peasant, having conceived a plan to assassinate Napoleon. He saves a French officer’s life, rescues a child from a burning house, and is finally captured by the French as a suspected arsonist. While Petersburg society carries on almost as normal, Pierre is held prisoner with a group of Russian captives.

Book Fourteen: 1812 — The Partisan War

Russian guerrilla forces harry the French supply lines. Nicholas Rostov’s regiment operates behind enemy lines. Young Petya Rostov, barely sixteen, joins a partisan detachment and is shot dead in a skirmish — a sudden, senseless death that Tolstoy renders with heartbreaking economy. The partisan leader Denisov and the mysterious Dolokhov lead a raid that frees a group of French prisoners, including Pierre.

Book Fifteen: 1812–13 — Retreat and Death

The French army disintegrates on the road back through snow and cold. Pierre, weakened by captivity but inwardly transformed, marches with the prisoners. During his captivity he befriended the peasant soldier Platon Karataev — a man of serene, unconscious goodness who embodies for Pierre the meaning he has spent years searching for. Platon is shot by the French when he can no longer walk. Andrei, transported with the Rostov convoy, dies peacefully in Yaroslavl, reconciled with Natasha, who nurses him in his final weeks. Natasha and Princess Mary are drawn together by shared grief.

First Epilogue: 1813–20 — Peace

Seven years after the war, the characters have settled into new lives. Pierre and Natasha are married with four children; their union, while outwardly domestic, is full of shared intellectual purpose — Pierre now moves in Decembrist circles opposed to the Tsar. Nicholas and Princess Mary are married and managing the Bolkonsky estate; their marriage is loving but not without friction. Sonya lives quietly with the family, her sacrifice unrecognized. Denisov visits for the holidays. The next generation begins to stir: Andrei’s son, young Nicholas Bolkonsky, idolizes his dead father and listens, wide-eyed, to Pierre’s talk of duty and justice.

Second Epilogue — History and Free Will

Tolstoy abandons narrative entirely for a lengthy philosophical essay. He argues that conventional history, by attributing events to the will of great men such as Napoleon or Alexander, fundamentally misunderstands causation. The real motive force of history is the sum total of millions of individual wills, none of which, individually, is free. Just as calculus resolves Zeno’s paradox of Achilles and the tortoise by treating motion as continuous rather than a series of discontinuous steps, so a true philosophy of history must move beyond the “great man” theory and grapple with the infinitely small increments of human action.

Major Themes

War and Peace

The title is not merely a contrast between two states of affairs but a sustained meditation on the relationship between them. Tolstoy shows war as chaos, waste, and horror — yet also as the crucible in which characters discover their deepest selves. The “peace” sections reveal that drawing-room life can be as hollow and destructive as the battlefield.

History and the Individual

Tolstoy is skeptical of the idea that history is made by exceptional individuals. Napoleon, surrounded by the apparatus of genius, is shown as a man carried along by forces he does not control. Kutuzov’s greatness, by contrast, lies in his willingness to not act — to wait, to yield Moscow, to trust that time and Russia’s vastness will do what no order of battle can.

Free Will and Necessity

Throughout the novel — and at length in the Second Epilogue — Tolstoy wrestles with determinism. Every character feels themselves to be acting freely, yet looking back on their choices (and Tolstoy’s narrator looking forward) each action seems inevitable. The novel offers no resolution, only the honest acknowledgement of the paradox.

Spiritual Seeking

Pierre’s journey — from dissolute young count through Freemasonry, disillusionment, captivity, and the transformative friendship with Platon Karataev — is the novel’s central spiritual arc. The answer he eventually finds is not doctrinal but experiential: meaning lies in simple, present, loving attention to life.

The Natural vs. the Artificial

Tolstoy repeatedly contrasts authentic experience (Natasha dancing a folk dance, Kutuzov sleeping through a council of war, Pierre watching the night sky from a prisoner’s cart) with the hollow performance of society (Anna Pávlovna’s soirées, Helene’s salon, Napoleon’s theatrical posturing before the Battle of Borodino).
